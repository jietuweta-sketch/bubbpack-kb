Innova Market Insights named "Substantiated Sustainability" its number one packaging trend for 2026. The framing is careful, and it matters: not sustainability, but *substantiated* sustainability. Consumers still say they will pay more for greener packaging. Innova's survey puts the figure at 85% who are willing to pay a premium. But the same research shows the trust is thin. Mondi's latest e-commerce report found 90% of European shoppers prefer recyclable, right-sized packaging, yet only half say they trust the sustainability claims brands actually print on the box.

That gap, between what people say they want and what they believe, is now a legal problem, not just a marketing one. Two EU rules landed within weeks of each other in 2026. The Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR, Regulation 2025/40) went live on 12 August. The Empowering Consumers for the Green Transition Directive (EmpCo, 2024/825) follows on 27 September. Together they do something simple and overdue: they make an unsubstantiated green claim on packaging an unfair commercial practice, with a penalty floor set at 4% of annual turnover per member state.

## The claims are finally getting checked

For more than a decade, "recyclable," "eco-friendly," and "green" have been loose words on packaging. Innova tracked new food and beverage launches from October 2020 to September 2025 and found "recyclable" was the single most common environmental claim, appearing on 38% of them. What it meant in practice varied wildly. A material can be chemically recyclable in a lab and still never get collected, sorted, or reprocessed in the places where it is actually sold.

That is exactly the distinction the new rules draw. EmpCo bans vague environmental claims and self-made labels. A bare "recyclable" stamp becomes misleading if the customer has no realistic way to recycle the thing where they live. The compliant version names the material and the route: "cardboard outer, recyclable in kerbside paper collection" or "LDPE mailer, check local soft-plastic points." Specificity is no longer a best practice. It is the difference between a defensible claim and a 4% fine.

What makes this harder than most compliance changes is that the two laws work from opposite directions. PPWR is product law. It regulates what the packaging physically is and how it may be labelled. EmpCo is marketing law. It regulates what you may say about the packaging. A box has to satisfy both at once, and satisfying one does not excuse the other.

## What "recyclable" now has to mean

Under PPWR, all packaging placed on the EU market must be recyclable. But that requirement phases in on a schedule, and the schedule is where brands get caught out. "Designed for recycling" criteria apply from 1 January 2030. "Recyclable at scale" applies from 1 January 2035. The gap between the two is real: a pack can be designed to be recycled but still fail in practice because the sorting infrastructure does not exist where it is sold.

There are hard numbers attached. Plastic packaging must hit minimum post-consumer recycled content targets, starting with 30% for single-use PET beverage bottles by 2030, and more for other categories by 2035. Transport, grouped, and e-commerce packaging cannot be more than 50% empty space. These are not aspirations. They are thresholds that decide whether a format can legally stay on the market.

The economics shift with them. Extended producer responsibility fees across the EU are increasingly "eco-modulated," meaning lightweight, mono-material, readily recyclable formats pay less and hard-to-recycle formats pay progressively more. A flexible film pouch can be technically recyclable and still land in the highest fee tier, because the fee prices what actually happens in a real material recovery facility, not what the resin is capable of. Paper and fibre consistently price below plastic film under every state-level program built so far.

The upshot is that recyclability has stopped being a claim you make and become a design decision you make early. Retro-fitting is expensive. Choosing a mono-material structure, avoiding multi-layer laminates, and right-sizing the box from the first order is cheap by comparison, and it is the only route that survives both the letter of the law and the scepticism of the customer reading the box.

## What this means for smaller brands

Most of the compliance conversation is written for corporations with packaging engineers and legal teams. Small and mid-size brands hear "4% of turnover" and "design for recycling" and quietly panic, or quietly ignore it. Ignoring it is the worse option, because the people most exposed to greenwashing accusations are not the giants. They are the brands selling a handful of SKUs on Shopify, printing "eco-friendly" on a mailer because a supplier told them to, and shipping into a couple of EU markets without registering for extended producer responsibility in any of them.

The good news is that for a small brand, the fix is mostly a sourcing decision, not a compliance department. Pick formats that are recyclable by design out of the gate: mono-material boxes, paper-based mailers, minimal void space, no mixed-material laminates you cannot defend. Print the claim you can actually prove and nothing more. If you cannot name the recycling route, leave the word "recyclable" off the box.

Small-batch production changes the math here in your favour. A brand that orders a few hundred units at a time can test a mono-material structure or a right-sized carton on a real run and change it next month if the claim does not hold. A brand locked into a 5,000-unit minimum is stuck with whatever the first guess was. ## What to do before the year ends

The EmpCo deadline lands 27 September 2026. If you sell into the EU, this is the moment to read every environmental word on your packaging as if a regulator is standing behind the customer. Ask three questions. Can I name the material and the recycling route for this claim? Is the format actually recyclable where my customers live? And what does it cost me in producer fees if it is not?

Sustainability has moved from a marketing edge to a compliance baseline, and from a baseline to a design input. The brands that treat it as a sourcing decision made on the first order, not a sticker added on the last one, are the ones that keep the shelf, the margin, and the trust.
