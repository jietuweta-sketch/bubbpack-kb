# Packaging Compliance in 2026: What EPR and PPWR Mean for Small-Batch Brands

If your brand ships into the European Union, or into one of the US states that now charges for packaging, your box just became a filing requirement. Not a design choice. A line item on your balance sheet.

For most of the past decade, sustainability in packaging was something brands mentioned on the "about us" page and forgot about in procurement. That era is over. Matthew Rogerson of The Pack Scout put it plainly: the industry is moving from aspiration to accountability. The new rules don't care whether you have a compliance department or a single founder packing orders on the kitchen table.

## What actually changed in 2026

Two big shifts, on two continents.

In Europe, the Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation, usually shortened to PPWR, replaces the old Packaging Waste Directive. The difference matters. A directive let each member state write its own version. A regulation applies directly, one rulebook with no national wiggle room. The date everyone has circled is 12 August 2026, when the first wave of obligations kicks in.

What does PPWR actually require? A few things that sound fine in principle and get complicated in practice. Packaging has to be designed for recycling and, from 2030, hit a minimum recyclability score of 70%. Plastic packaging has to contain set percentages of recycled content by 2030 and 2040. Some formats carry reuse and refill targets. And there's a new labeling layer meant to tell consumers which bin the thing goes in.

The part most small brands miss is the documentation. PPWR isn't only about using recyclable materials. It's about being able to prove it, with a technical file and data from your packaging supplier. If you've ever bought boxes from a printer who gave you a price and a lead time and nothing else, that's the gap PPWR is about to expose.

Meanwhile, in the United States, Extended Producer Responsibility has stopped being a policy term. Seven states now make brands report the packaging they ship into the state and pay a fee based on it. Oregon, Colorado, California, Minnesota, and Maryland are all in. The fees are structured to reward recyclable, lightweight packaging and penalize the stuff that's hard to process. A brand that ships the same mailer everywhere suddenly has to know, state by state, what they owe and what they file.

## Why small brands get hit harder

Here's the part that doesn't make it into the conference keynotes: the compliance burden doesn't scale down with your order size.

A consumer-goods giant has a packaging team, a compliance department, and software that tracks material composition and recycled content across every SKU. A small DTC brand has one person who's also doing customer service and running ads. When Oregon asks for a report, or a distributor in Germany asks for recyclability documentation, that person is googling "EPR filing" at midnight.

The fee structure makes it worse. EPR fees and PPWR recyclability scoring both favor packaging designed cleanly from the start, mono-materials, minimal coatings, no mixed-material laminates that can't be separated. Large brands rebuilt their packaging around these rules over several years. Small brands are often still in whatever box the supplier happened to have, and switching feels like a project they don't have time for.

But the cost of not switching is real and it compounds. Non-compliance in the EU can get your product blocked from the market. In the US it means penalties and, in some states, restricted access. You can't skip the filing just because your volume is small.

## The practical fix: start with the box, not the spreadsheet

The good news is that most of this lands on a few decisions you can make at the packaging stage, before you're deep in reporting.

Start with the material. Recyclable in practice usually means mono-material, one type of paper or plastic, no foil lamination, no coatings that are hard to separate. It also means looking at the whole package, the ink, the adhesive, the label, the closure, and asking whether they help or hurt the recycling stream.

Then get the data. This is where small brands stall. Ask your packaging vendor for material composition, recycled content percentages, and whether the structure meets a recyclability standard, 4evergreen for paper, APR guidance for plastics in the US. If the vendor can't tell you, that's the answer. Under PPWR you need this in a technical file, and under US EPR you need it for your filings.

And keep it light. Lighter packaging costs less to ship and, under most EPR fee structures, costs less to comply with. Less material, lower freight, fewer headaches.

For a small brand, the temptation is to wait until the filing is due and then panic. The brands that handle this well treat packaging as part of the product launch, not an afterthought. They spec the box, the mailer, and the label at the same time they spec the product, and they ask the compliance questions up front.

## The short version

Packaging compliance stopped being optional in 2026. The EU's PPWR and a growing list of US EPR programs mean the box you ship is now a regulated product with data, fees, and deadlines attached.

None of this has to sink you. Most of the requirements come down to using recyclable materials, keeping packaging minimal, and being able to document what's in it. The brands that get caught are the ones that never asked their supplier for that information in the first place.
