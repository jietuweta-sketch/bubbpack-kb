# Extended Producer Responsibility in 2026: What Packaging Fees Mean for Small Brands

For most of the past decade, the real cost of a box ended at the supplier's invoice. You paid for the board, the print, the freight, and that was it. Whatever happened to the box after your customer opened it was somebody else's problem.

That arrangement is ending, state by state, and 2026 is the year the invoices start arriving. Maine has moved its extended producer responsibility (EPR) program into the startup-fee phase this year, with full implementation set for 2027. Oregon, Colorado, and Minnesota are not far behind, and more state bills are piling up. If you sell physical products in the US, the packaging you buy is about to carry a second, ongoing price tag that you have never seen on a purchase order.

The part that catches most founders off guard is not the fee itself. It is the discovery that "producer" does not mean what they assumed it meant.

## The fee is now tied to your material choices, not just your volume

Under EPR, the money you owe is calculated from two things: how much packaging you put into the market, and how recyclable that material actually is. The first number you already know. The second one is where most brands are quietly out of position.

A poly mailer with a single-use plastic window, a lamination that looks premium but cannot be separated, a box coated with something that contaminates the recycling stream: these all push your per-unit fee up, sometimes sharply. The incentives are not subtle. States that have written fee reductions into their laws, like Maine, are rewarding packaging that uses less material and recycles cleanly. In practice, that means a brand making deliberate choices about board weight, coatings, and adhesives pays a measurably different rate than one that defaulted to whatever the factory suggested.

This is the first real shift worth understanding: sustainability stopped being a marketing line and became a line item. You cannot tweet your way out of an EPR assessment. The fee looks at the box, not the brand story.

If you sell into Europe as well, the same logic is already in motion there. The EU's Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation is pushing brands toward recyclability targets with real deadlines, and the big consumer goods companies have been reworking their packaging specs for over a year to get ahead of it. The direction is the same on both sides of the Atlantic: the material your package is made of now has a direct, auditable cost attached to it.

## "Producer" includes you, even if you are small

Here is the trap that trips up DTC founders and small brands every time these laws go live. You do not need to own a factory to be a producer. If your name is on the package, or you are the one placing it into the market, the law counts you.

There is a small-producer threshold in most states, and yes, some brands fall under it. But here is the fine print that the exemption headlines tend to bury: being under the threshold usually gets you out of paying fees, not out of filing. You still have to register and report your packaging data. Miss that, and you are in default before anyone has charged you a cent.

Oregon and Minnesota go further. They require essentially all producers to join the producer responsibility organization, which makes the "go it alone" path mostly theoretical. Even a pure-play online shop shipping from a garage needs to register, report, and pay where it applies.

None of this is meant to be cruel. The policy goal is straightforward: make the people who put packaging into circulation responsible for what happens to it at the end. But the reporting burden lands hardest on the people least staffed to handle it, which is the smaller operator with no compliance department.

## What you can actually do about it, before the invoice lands

The good news is that the levers are concrete, and they mostly live in your packaging spec, which you control.

Start with materials. Mono-material structures recycle more cleanly than laminated ones, and they attract lower EPR rates. If your box or mailer uses a single substrate, say so, and keep coatings and adhesives to what the recycling stream can actually process. This is not an aesthetic trade-off anymore. It is arithmetic.

Then look at weight and fit. You pay fees on material volume, so a box that is 30% air is 30% wasted fee. Right-sizing your packaging cuts both the freight cost and the EPR assessment at once. It is one of the few places in the business where the same change lowers two separate expenses. The carriers are pushing the same direction from their side: USPS is changing its dimensional weight divisor in July 2026, so oversized boxes that were merely wasteful are about to get more expensive to ship on top of everything else.

Finally, track your data now. EPR reporting asks for material type, weight, and volume by category, and retroactively rebuilding that from a pile of supplier invoices is miserable. A small, repeatable spec document, kept current, saves you a weekend of spreadsheet archaeology later.

Extended producer responsibility is not a future risk. In 2026 it is a current cost, and it rewards brands that get their packaging spec right while punishing those that never looked at it.
