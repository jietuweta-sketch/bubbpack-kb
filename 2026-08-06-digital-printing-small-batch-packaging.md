I still remember talking to a skincare founder in 2023 who had 300 units of product sitting in her apartment and a quote for 5,000 custom boxes she couldn't afford. She ended up buying plain kraft boxes and hand-stamping her logo on each one. That was the state of custom packaging for small brands: you either ordered at industrial scale or you didn't get custom at all.

Three years later the math looks different.

Digital printing didn't suddenly get cheaper in 2025. It crossed a threshold where the per-unit economics stopped punishing small runs, and that changes everything for DTC brands that launch with hundreds of units instead of tens of thousands.

## The old math: why traditional printing hates small orders

Flexographic and offset printing are fast once the press is running. The catch is everything that has to happen before the press moves.

A flexo job needs plates. Each color in your design needs its own plate, and each plate costs between $200 and $600 depending on complexity and press size. A four-color box design means four plates. Before you've printed a single box, you're already $800 to $2,400 in the hole. Then there's setup time: mounting plates, registering colors, dialing in ink density. A press operator might spend 45 minutes getting a job ready, and that time costs the same whether the run is 500 boxes or 50,000.

Offset is similar. Plates, makeready, ink-water balance calibration. The fixed cost per job sits somewhere between $400 and $1,000 before the first sheet feeds.

This is why converters set minimum order quantities at 1,000, 3,000, or 5,000 units. Below that, the plate and setup cost eats the margin entirely. Nobody is losing money on purpose; the equipment just was not built for short runs.

Small brands either paid for boxes they did not need (and stored them, sometimes for years) or gave up on custom packaging altogether.

## What digital printing actually changes

Digital presses skip the plates. A digital press — HP Indigo, Konica Minolta, Xeikon, or a high-end inkjet system — images directly onto the substrate from a digital file. No plates, no makeready in the traditional sense, no color registration dance. The first sheet off a digital press looks the same as the ten-thousandth.

This eliminates the bulk of the fixed cost. Setup drops from hundreds of dollars to essentially zero. Per-unit cost is higher than offset at large volumes — digital toner and inkjet ink cost more per impression than offset ink — but at small volumes the total is dramatically lower.

Some rough numbers from converters I have talked to:

A 500-unit run of custom folding cartons with four-color printing might cost $2.80 to $4.50 per box on an offset press once you amortize plates and setup. The same job on a digital press lands between $3.50 and $6.00 per box, but the minimum order drops to 50 or 100 units. For a brand launching with 300 units, total packaging cost goes from "not possible" to "a line item."

The tradeoff is finish quality on certain stocks. Digital toner sits on top of the paper rather than soaking in like offset ink, so metallic and uncoated papers sometimes show a sheen difference. But for standard coated boards — the SBS and CCNB substrates most folding cartons use — the gap is narrow enough that consumers do not notice.

## What this means if you are launching a DTC brand

The obvious win is that you can order packaging that matches your actual production run. No more pallets of boxes in a garage. But there are three less obvious shifts worth paying attention to:

**Iteration gets cheaper.** When a print job has no setup cost, you can test a design with 50 units, get feedback from customers or retail buyers, and revise. Traditional printing made packaging design feel like a one-shot decision because reordering meant paying plates all over again. Digital makes packaging iterative in a way that web design went iterative 15 years ago.

**Seasonal and limited-edition runs become practical.** A brand that sells 2,000 units a year can now do a 200-unit holiday box, a 150-unit collab design, a 100-unit event-specific run — all with different artwork and no plate charges. This was unthinkable under the old model unless your margins were massive.

**Sustainability gets easier to act on, not just talk about.** Overproduction is the packaging industry's dirty secret. Brands over-order boxes because the unit price drops at higher quantities, and a meaningful percentage of those boxes end up in recycling without ever holding a product. Digital's low minimums mean you order what you need, and if demand shifts, you are not stuck with obsolete packaging.

## The numbers that matter if you are comparing options

If you are pricing packaging for a new product line, here is what determines whether digital or traditional makes sense:

- **Under 500 units:** Digital almost always wins on total cost. The plate savings alone cover the higher per-unit print cost.
- **500 to 2,000 units:** It depends on complexity. Simple designs with one or two spot colors can go either way. Four-color process with variable data (serial numbers, QR codes, personalized elements) favors digital regardless of quantity.
- **Above 5,000 units:** Traditional printing usually wins on per-unit cost, assuming you can absorb the upfront plate and setup charges.

Structure and finishing — die-cutting, folding, gluing — cost about the same on both paths. The print method does not change how a box is physically constructed. Your main decision is just about the printing economics.

One thing to watch: some converters quote digital prices that look competitive at 1,000 units but are actually running offset and charging a small-format premium. Ask whether the job is digital or offset and whether plates are being made. If plates are involved, you are paying for them whether the line item says so or not.

## Where this is heading

The HP Indigo 100K and similar presses keep pushing digital speeds closer to offset territory. Toner costs have been dropping roughly 3 to 5 percent a year, so the crossover point where offset becomes cheaper keeps moving further out on the volume axis. Some converters now run digital all the way to 10,000 or 15,000 impressions before they bother switching to offset.

Add in the fact that more brands are launching DTC-first — no retail buyer demanding a 10,000-unit purchase order — and the market is tilting toward smaller, more frequent packaging orders as the default, not the exception.

The 500-box minimum is not coming back. If you are still getting quotes that start at 3,000 units, you are talking to the wrong suppliers.
