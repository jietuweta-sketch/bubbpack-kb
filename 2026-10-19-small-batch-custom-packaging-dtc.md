For years, custom packaging worked like a velvet rope at a club. If your brand ordered 500 or 1,000 units, you got in. If you were a small direct-to-consumer brand that needed 60 boxes for a first product launch, you got turned away or quoted a price that made no sense.

That gate is coming down, and the numbers show how fast. The global custom packaging market hit $48.17 billion in 2025 and is headed to $50.63 billion in 2026, according to Fortune Business Insights. Smithers put the ecommerce packaging market at $78.4 billion in 2025, growing toward $105.2 billion by 2031. But the more telling shift is underneath those figures: who can now afford custom packaging, and how they find it.

## Why minimum order quantities used to strangle small brands

The old economics were simple and brutal. Offset printing needs plates, and plates need setup, and setup only pays for itself when you run thousands of identical boxes. So converters drew a line at 500 to 1,000 units and small brands stayed on the wrong side of it.

The irony is that small brands knew exactly what they were missing. A UCanPack survey found 72% of small businesses say custom packaging improves their brand image, but 68% avoid it anyway because of high minimum order quantities. They wanted the branded box. They just couldn't buy it in a quantity that matched their reality.

It was worse than a flat rejection, too. For brands ordering under 10,000 units, setup and tooling fees ate up 8% to 15% of the total packaging cost, according to Margin Lab data compiled by TruePack Global. A small founder paid the full freight of fixed costs spread over a tiny run, while a large brand spread the same cost over millions of pieces. The system didn't just prefer big buyers. It was built around them.

## What digital short runs actually change

Digital printing flips that logic. No plates, no long setup, no reason to force a 500-unit minimum on someone who needs 50. The per-unit cost is higher than a huge offset run, but the total is lower because you only pay for what you print, and you skip the tooling fees entirely.

For a DTC brand, that changes the math on risk. A founder can test packaging on a real product run instead of betting a warehouse of boxes on a design that hasn't sold yet. When something flops, the leftover inventory is a few dozen boxes, not a pallet.

Speed matters just as much as quantity. Standard digital runs can ship in a few days after proof approval, which means packaging stops being a two-month lead-time problem and starts being a "same week" decision. And the sustainability story is stronger than it sounds: FSC-certified paper, plant-based cushioning, and single-material construction that actually recycles cleanly at the curb.

The return on that spend is measurable, too. Dotcom Distribution found 52% of consumers are likely to buy again from a retailer that ships in premium packaging, and VistaPrint's 2025 survey of 1,000 U.S. shoppers put it bluntly: 96% said packaging quality affects how much they trust a brand, and 69% had received a poorly packed order that lowered their opinion of it. Half of shoppers share unboxing moments, and for Gen Z packaging is the number one reason to post. That's free reach attached to a box you already had to buy.

## Where buyers find packaging now

The discovery side of this market has moved, and most packaging suppliers haven't followed it. PYMNTS Intelligence found ChatGPT jumped from 2% to 30% as a product-search platform in about two years, and 47% of online shoppers used AI somewhere in their most recent purchase journey. During the 2025 holiday season, Salesforce reported AI and agents influenced 20% of all retail sales, driving $262 billion in revenue, with AI-referred shoppers converting nine times more often than social-media referrals.

That shift reaches packaging buyers, not just consumers. A founder launching a skincare line no longer starts by typing "custom boxes" into a search bar and wading through ten directories. They ask an assistant "where can I get 100 branded boxes for my product, recyclable, under two weeks" and expect a direct answer. Suppliers that describe themselves clearly, structure their content for machine reading, and state their minimums and turnaround plainly are the ones the assistants cite.

For a small brand, this is the same lesson from both sides of the fence. The packaging itself got cheaper and more flexible. The way customers find you changed at the same time. A brand that still thinks of packaging as a fixed, high-minimum cost is competing against founders who treat it as a fast, low-risk marketing asset they can turn around in a week.

## What this means if you're launching something

None of this requires a big budget. It requires dropping the assumption that custom packaging is an enterprise-only expense.

Start with the real constraint: your order size, not a converter's preferred run. If you need 80 units for a launch, find a supplier whose system is built around 80 units rather than one that makes an exception for you. Ask about materials up front, because recyclability is now a stated buying factor for 90% of consumers, per Shorr Packaging's 2025 survey. And check turnaround before you care about anything else, since a box that arrives after your launch is worse than no box.
