# Connected Packaging: How a QR Code Turns Your Box Into a Marketing Channel

A DTC founder I talked to last month told me his repeat-purchase problem the wrong way around. He thought he needed more traffic. What he actually had was a data gap: every sale ended at the doorstep, and he never learned who his customers were, what they liked, or how to reach them again without paying an ad platform for the privilege.

The box he shipped was the one surface his customer touched with their hands, and it was dead. A plain mailer with a printed logo. Meanwhile, 92% of CPG brands now put a QR code on their packaging, according to Packaging Strategies, and 2.2 billion people worldwide scan QR codes regularly. The gap between those two numbers is where small brands lose customers they already paid to acquire.

Connected packaging is just a name for closing that gap. You put a code on the package, the customer scans it, and the box becomes a two-way channel instead of a one-way shipment. The market is growing at about 8% a year and is projected to reach $57.4 billion by 2034, per Fortune Business Insights. The interesting part for a small brand is that the entry cost is a printed square, not a software team.

## The scan is already a habit

The thing that changed in the last few years is that consumers stopped needing to be taught what a QR code does. In the US, 102.6 million smartphone users are projected to scan one in 2026, roughly one in three Americans, according to eMarketer. Uniqode's State of QR Codes report, built on 188 million scans and surveys of a thousand consumers, found 44% of people scan weekly or daily, and only 8% have never scanned one.

What they scan for is the part most brands get wrong. The top reason is not a discount. It's information. 75% of consumers scan to get more information, ahead of 52% who scan for offers and 35% for payments. In other words, your customer already wants to learn something about the product they just bought. A code that drops them on a blank homepage answers nothing and gets closed in three seconds.

The people doing this well treat the code as the front door to something worth the scan. Lipton puts sourcing stories and brewing guides behind its tea packaging. Nestlé uses codes for traceability, letting you follow coffee beans from farm to cup. Coca-Cola runs location-specific promotions off a single dynamic code. None of these are gimmicks. Each one answers a question the customer actually has, or hands them something useful in the moment they're holding the product.

There's a smaller-brand version of every one of those. You don't need a blockchain partner to tell a customer which batch their candle came from, or a loyalty app to offer a repeat-order code. You need a working code and a page that respects the person on the other end.

## What to put behind the scan

The rule that separates useful connected packaging from a wasted square is simple: reward the scan, don't just repeat the label. If the page behind your code is your website homepage, you've added a step and nothing else. The customer already found you. The scan has to give them something they couldn't get by reading the box.

A few things that earn their keep for small brands:

**Usage or care content.** The top scanning motivation is information, so put the information people actually need. For a food product, storage and shelf life. For skincare, how much to use and when. A code that answers the question the customer was about to google anyway is the cheapest goodwill you'll ever buy.

**First-party data, asked for plainly.** Around 80% of consumers are willing to share data through a scan interaction, per Uniqode. The honest version is a short form that trades something real for an email: a repeat-order discount, early access, a recipe, a warranty registration. You get a customer you can reach without paying Meta every time, and they get something concrete in return.

**Proof of authenticity.** If you make anything where fakes exist, a scannable batch or serial number is a trust signal you can't fake as easily as a logo. This matters more for supplements, electronics, and anything people put on their skin. 64% of shoppers have scanned a code on a product in a store, per World Sync, often precisely to check that the thing in their hand is real.

**A next step that fits the moment.** After someone scans, they're engaged and holding the product. That's the moment to ask for a review, a referral, or a follow-up order. Not in the shipping email three days later, but right there. The conversion window is short and the intent is high.

None of this requires a developer. A dynamic QR code, which you can point at different pages without reprinting, accounts for 65% of global QR code revenue already, per Mordor Intelligence. The brand-specific part is just deciding what the customer deserves to find.

## Where the code itself fails

The data on failed scans is uncomfortable because almost all of it is preventable. Uniqode's survey found the top complaints are a code that doesn't scan (36%), an expired or dead link (29%), and a slow or broken page (27%). Three out of four failures come down to print quality and link hygiene, not strategy.

The print side is easy to get wrong on packaging specifically. A code squeezed into the corner of a busy label, printed too small or in low contrast, is a code that won't scan when the light is bad. The fix is boring but real: keep the code large enough, give it clear quiet space, test it on the actual material and finish, and test again after the design goes to print. A glossy finish that looks premium can also throw off a phone camera's focus.

The link side is a discipline problem. Static codes that point to a page you later moved, or a campaign you ended without redirecting, are dead codes wearing your brand. A dynamic code avoids the reprint problem, but only if someone keeps the redirects alive. If you run a seasonal promotion behind the code, point it somewhere useful when the season ends instead of leaving a 404.

There's a timing consideration worth knowing. GS1, the standards body behind the UPC barcode, is pushing 2D codes like QR into retail point-of-sale alongside traditional barcodes, with the transition expected to settle by the end of 2027. You don't need to do anything about that today, but a brand already comfortable with QR on its packaging will absorb the shift without a redesign scramble.

## The box is your only owned channel

What makes connected packaging land for small brands, specifically, is that it fixes a structural disadvantage. A big brand has a retail endcap, a media budget, and a CRM. You have a box and a customer. The box is the one touchpoint you control end to end, and the code is what makes it collect information instead of just carrying product.

That's the whole pitch for thinking about it now. You already pay to ship the box. The marginal cost of turning it into a channel is a printed code and a page worth landing on.
