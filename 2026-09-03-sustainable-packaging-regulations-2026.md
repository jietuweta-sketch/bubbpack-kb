# Sustainable Packaging Regulations 2026: What Small Brands Actually Need to Do

For most of the last decade, "sustainable packaging" was a line you printed on a box and moved on. That window is closing. Starting August 2026, most of the EU's Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR) takes effect, and it treats vague green claims as a compliance problem rather than a branding choice.

Here's the part that catches small brands off guard. The big rules were drafted with multinationals in mind, but the obligations flow down. If you sell into the EU, into the UK, or into US states that have written their own laws, the packaging on your product is now regulated in a way it wasn't two years ago. A "planet-friendly" label you added because it sounded good can now be a liability instead of a selling point.

Let me walk through what actually changed, and what you can do about it without hiring a compliance team.

## Why "trust us" packaging is dead

The core shift isn't that sustainability matters more. It always mattered. The shift is that unverified claims now carry real cost and legal risk.

The EU's PPWR, which entered force in February 2025, requires brands to back environmental claims with evidence and pushes hard on recyclability, recycled content, and reducing overall packaging. It also sets minimum recycled-content targets for plastic packaging that ramp up through 2030 and 2040. The UK's Competition and Markets Authority has taken a similar line on green claims in consumer goods. In the US, the FTC is revising its Green Guides for the first time in more than a decade, and states including California and Oregon are writing their own rules.

Consumer sentiment moved the same direction. GreenPrint's annual Business of Sustainability Index keeps finding that younger buyers, the 18-to-34 crowd especially, are deeply skeptical of unverified claims. Words like "eco-friendly" and "planet-friendly" now read as noise rather than reassurance. If your packaging says "100% sustainable" and the claim can't be traced, you're not convincing anyone, and in some markets you're technically mislabeling.

The practical takeaway: specific beats vague. "Recyclable FSC-certified paperboard" is a claim you can stand behind and prove with a spec sheet. "Eco-friendly" is a risk you don't need.

## Extended producer responsibility is landing, one market at a time

The other big piece is Extended Producer Responsibility, or EPR. Under EPR, the cost of collecting and recycling packaging shifts from taxpayers to the producers who put it on shelves. France, Germany, Spain, and a growing list of US states already run EPR programs, with more scheduled through the late 2020s.

For a small brand, EPR usually shows up as a registration and a fee. You register with the relevant scheme in each market where you sell, report your packaging volume, and pay a share of the recycling cost. The fees are modest at small scale, but the admin adds up, and the rules differ from one jurisdiction to the next.

This is where material choice stops being a preference and becomes arithmetic. In most EPR schemes your fee is lower if your packaging is genuinely recyclable and lighter. Paper-based boxes that recycle easily tend to score better than multi-material laminates or finishes coated in plastic film. A simple kraft mailer box printed with water-based ink is about as friendly to EPR scoring as packaging gets. A box laminated with plastic on every surface is not.

None of this means you need to redesign everything tomorrow. It means the packaging you pick now quietly determines what you'll pay and whether you'll have a compliance headache later. The brands that treat this as a design constraint from day one are the ones who won't have to rip and replace in 2027.

## The good news: sustainable short runs are finally affordable

Here's what changed on the supply side, and it's genuinely good news for small brands.

Five years ago, "custom recyclable packaging" and "small order" didn't belong in the same sentence. Digital printing and flexible die-cutting have closed that gap. Minimum orders that used to sit at 5,000 or 10,000 units are now commonly 50 or 100, and single-unit samples are easy to get. The global custom packaging market was worth about $48 billion in 2025 and is forecast to nearly double by 2034, and a large share of that growth is coming from small and DTC brands ordering short runs.

What that means in practice: you can order a few hundred recyclable boxes, test a design, and reorder without being stuck with a warehouse full of the wrong thing. You can also afford to right-size, which is one of the cheapest sustainability wins available. A box that fits the product instead of shipping air cuts material use, lowers freight cost, and scores better on EPR all at once. Those three benefits come from the same single decision.

## What to do this month

If you sell physical product, a short list covers most of it.

Check where you actually sell. If the EU, the UK, or an EPR state sits anywhere in your customer base, the rules apply to you even if your business is registered elsewhere.

Write down what your packaging is made of. If you can't name the material, your supplier can. If your supplier can't, find one who can.

Replace vague claims with specific ones. Swap "eco-friendly" for the actual material and certification, and only say "recyclable" if it holds true for the recycling infrastructure where your customers live.

Right-size your boxes and consider paper-based stock with water-based or soy-based ink. These moves lower both your EPR fees and your freight bill.

Keep a record. The days of printing a claim with no file behind it are over. A one-page spec sheet is enough at small scale, but you need something.

## The short version

Sustainability in packaging has gone from a nice-to-have to a compliance category with teeth. The EU's PPWR starts biting in earnest in August 2026, EPR schemes are spreading, and consumers have learned to ignore unverifiable green claims. That sounds like bad news, and for brands still printing "eco-friendly" on plastic laminate it is. For everyone else it's an opportunity to do less and say it honestly.
