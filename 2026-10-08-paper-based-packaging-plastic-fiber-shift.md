The paper and board packaging market passed $440 billion in 2025. Forecasters expect it to hit $691 billion by 2034. That headline number is the boring part. The interesting story is what sits inside it: molded fiber roughly doubling, e-commerce boxes and mailers climbing from $34.5 billion to $72.7 billion, while several rigid plastic formats go in the other direction.

Paper is no longer the cheap fallback. It is taking jobs that used to belong to plastic and aluminum, and it is doing it because the material got better, not because anyone forced the issue. If you sell a physical product, this shift is going to reach your shelf one way or another.

## The plastic formats losing ground

The declines are specific, which is what makes them credible. Expanded foam protective packaging is forecast to drop from $3.2 billion to $0.7 billion. Insulated shippers and cold-chain boxes fall from $2.3 billion to $0.8 billion. Plastic and laminate squeeze tubes slide from $2.9 billion to $0.8 billion.

Meanwhile the fiber side is picking up the load. E-commerce boxes and mailers are the fastest-growing slice, more than doubling over the same window. Paper bags and sacks nearly double too.

None of this means plastic is disappearing. Rigid plastic packaging still grows, just slower than paper on every horizon the analysts track. What is happening is more surgical: the formats with no real end-of-life story are being swapped out first. Foam peanuts and EPS inserts were always landfill fodder. A corrugated box with a molded fiber insert can go in the same bin as a cereal box.

## The real change is in barriers

For years the honest objection to paper was performance. Paper absorbs grease, lets oxygen through, and goes limp when it gets wet. That objection is eroding.

Tetra Pak put €60 million into a pilot plant in Lund, Sweden, to develop a paper-based barrier that replaces the thin aluminum layer in aseptic beverage cartons. A carton using that barrier, made with Lactogal, cuts the carbon footprint of milk packaging by roughly a third.

Mondi won nine WorldStar Packaging Awards in 2026 on the strength of paper innovation. Its FreshFood BOX replaces expanded polystyrene for chilled grocery delivery with a fully recyclable, temperature-controlled paper alternative. Smurfit Westrock now ships paper punnets for fresh produce that are renewable, biodegradable, and recyclable at home.

The common thread is barrier coatings. Bio-based, mineral, and aluminum-oxide coatings are letting paper handle moisture, grease, and oxygen in applications that used to be locked to plastic. That is the quiet technical breakthrough behind the market numbers.

It is also a global story, and a lopsided one. Asia Pacific accounts for 46% of paper and board demand at $203 billion, with China alone at $107 billion. Europe sits at $99.5 billion and North America at $94.3 billion. The paper transition is not a Western fad; it is the default material in the regions that already produce most of the world's packaging.

## Why a small brand should care now

Two forces are converging on you specifically.

First, regulation. The EU Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation made PFAS restrictions in food-contact packaging enforceable in August 2026. By 2030, all packaging on the EU market has to be designed to be recyclable. The days of slapping a "recyclable" label on a multi-layer plastic pouch are numbered.

Second, and honestly more immediate, your customers. A Wall Street Journal figure cited across the 2026 trend reports found that 42% of European shoppers and 37% of North American shoppers have already refused to buy a product because of its packaging. Paper reads as natural, recyclable, and easy to dispose of. It signals sustainability without needing a footnote explaining the resin code.

For a small brand, that matters more than for a global one. Unilever can fund a multi-year mono-material R&D program and issue a press release about it. You probably cannot. Paper is the path of least resistance. A corrugated mailer with a molded fiber insert is genuinely recyclable, genuinely cheap to source in low quantities, and instantly legible to a customer who just wants to know where the box goes after unboxing.

## Where to start

If you are watching this shift and wondering what to do, start with the single highest-volume item you ship. For most e-commerce brands that is the outer mailer or box, plus whatever keeps the product from rattling around inside.

Swap that one touchpoint to paper first. Measure the response: fewer complaints about "too much plastic," more unboxing photos, and — depending on your market — a cleaner line in any EPR or recyclability report you file. Then decide whether the rest of the packaging stack is worth the same treatment.

Do not over-engineer it. The brands winning on packaging right now are not the ones with the most elaborate inserts. They are the ones whose packaging disappears into the recycling bin without drama. Paper does that naturally.

## The bottom line

Paper-based packaging is the fastest-growing material story in the industry this decade, and the growth is coming from formats that small brands actually buy: mailers, boxes, molded fiber inserts. Regulation and consumer pressure both point the same direction, and the technical barriers that kept paper out of wet and greasy applications are falling.
