On August 12 this year, a rule went live across the EU that a surprising number of small food brands still have not looked at. Food packaging containing PFAS above set limits can no longer be placed on the European market. There is no grace period and no stock-exhaustion window, according to the European Commission's guidance. If you have grease-proof wrappers sitting in a warehouse today, they cannot legally ship to an EU customer. The ban has now been in force for about ten weeks.

PFAS are the chemicals that make pizza boxes, takeaway containers, bakery paper, and microwave popcorn bags repel oil and water. The "forever chemicals" nickname is not marketing: these compounds barely break down in the environment or in the human body. The Guardian has been tracking them for years across everything from non-stick cookware to dental floss. Food packaging was one of the last places they were still used at scale, and that is exactly where the new rules landed first.

## What the ban actually covers

The legal instrument is the EU Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation, formally Regulation (EU) 2025/40. It entered into force in February 2025 and began applying on 12 August 2026, and the PFAS restriction in food-contact packaging took effect on day one. The Commission published implementation guidance and an FAQ in March 2026, then finalized guidance in June 2026 confirming there is no phase-out period for packaging placed on the market after the application date.

This is a threshold-based ban, not an absolute zero. Intentionally added PFAS above defined limits is what gets a package pulled. In practice that targets the barrier coatings on grease-resistant paper and board: the liners in takeaway cups, the waxed-feel wrap on a sandwich, the coating inside a burger box. Anything that made cheap paper hold up against hot oil is now a compliance question.

The same regulation phases in more obligations over the next decade. Harmonized labelling rules arrive in August 2028. Recyclability by design, packaging minimization, and minimum recycled-content targets in plastic kick in from January 2030. Packaging must be recyclable at scale by 2035. For a food brand, PFAS is the thing you had to fix now; the rest is a runway you can plan against.

## Why this hits small brands harder than big ones

A large food manufacturer has a compliance team, a lab budget, and relationships with half a dozen converter suppliers. A small brand does not. The founder who buys 300 custom boxes for a granola or pastry launch is making that decision alone, usually without testing the coating on the paper they ordered.

The timing is not kind to small players either. The EU is not the only place tightening rules. Seven US states now run packaging extended-producer-responsibility programs: Maine, Oregon, Colorado, California, Minnesota, Maryland, and Washington. Colorado sent producers their first invoices in January 2026, and California's permanent SB 54 regulations took effect on 1 May 2026. India's amended Plastic Waste Management Rules set recycled-content targets that step up every year. The trend is the same in every market: the burden of proof is moving onto the brand, not the raw-material supplier.

For a small food brand, the practical question is simple. When you order boxes or wrappers, can you state, in writing, that the food-contact surface is PFAS-free? If your converter shrugs, you have a problem, because "I didn't know" is not a defense and the fines are not sized for a startup's budget.

The switch itself is not that hard technically. PFAS-free barrier coatings exist and work: water-based coatings, plant-based linings, and mono-material paper that needs no plastic film at all. What is hard is knowing whether the thing you actually received matches what was promised.

## How to switch without betting your launch budget

The first move is the cheapest one: ask the supplier for documentation. A real converter can give you a compliance statement naming the coating and confirming no intentionally added PFAS in the food-contact layer. If the answer is vague, treat it as a no.

Second, match the packaging to what the food actually needs. A dry snack in a sealed inner pouch does not need a grease-proof outer box. A hot, oily item does. When you stop defaulting to coated paper on everything, you cut the compliance surface down to the few items that genuinely need it.

Third, order small before you commit. Digital short-run printing means you can test a PFAS-free stock on a real run of 50 boxes instead of buying a pallet on faith. That matters here in a way it did not before, because a compliance mistake on a 5,000-unit offset run is an expensive problem, while the same mistake on a 60-box digital run is a lesson.
