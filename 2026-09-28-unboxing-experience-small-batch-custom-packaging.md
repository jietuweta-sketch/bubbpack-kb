A package lands on a customer's doorstep. Before they see the product, they see the box. For a direct-to-consumer brand, that box is the entire physical store: no shelf, no salesperson, no second chance to make a first impression. In a 2025 Mondi survey of 6,000 shoppers, 59% said packaging influenced whether they would buy from a brand again. Not whether they liked the product. Whether they came back.

That number is the quiet reason the unboxing moment has stopped being a nice-to-have and become a growth lever. Brands that treat the box as an afterthought are paying for it in repeat orders they never see.

## The box is doing the job advertising used to do

There is a structural shift happening that most founders miss because it happened slowly. When a customer buys online, the first time they touch anything physical from your brand is after they have already paid. The packaging has to carry the weight that a retail display, a shelf position, and a sales associate used to carry, all at once.

Josh Brooks, who runs the London Packaging Week program, put it plainly at this year's judges day: "Packaging is working a lot harder today than it has ever done before because it's picked up so much of the promotion side that advertising has lost."

For most DTC brands that is an uncomfortable truth. They spend heavily on ads to get the click, then ship the product in a plain brown mailer that does nothing to justify the price or build the relationship. The customer opens it, feels nothing, and the brand has spent real money to acquire someone it immediately underwhelms.

That same Mondi finding works in reverse. If 59% of people will reorder because of packaging, then a thoughtful box is not decoration. It is a retention tool with a measurable effect on lifetime value.

## Small batches are what make the moment affordable

The usual objection here is cost. Custom packaging, the thinking goes, means ordering five thousand units, paying for plates and setup, and sitting on a pallet of inventory while you figure out whether the design even works. For a brand doing a few hundred orders a month, that math does not close.

What has changed in the last couple of years is that low minimums stopped being the exception. A growing number of suppliers now start at dozens or low hundreds of units instead of thousands, and digital printing removed the plate fees that used to make short runs absurdly expensive. The unit economics flipped. A founder can order 200 boxes for a launch, see which design actually gets shared and reordered, then scale what works instead of committing to ten thousand units of a guess.

This matters more than most founders realize. Demand for a young DTC brand is unpredictable by definition. Cash tied up in a warehouse full of boxes is cash that cannot go into the next product or the next ad campaign. Small batches let you test packaging the same way you test creative: cheap, fast, and willing to kill what does not work.

Take a founder launching a new skincare line. She does not know yet whether the hero product will be the serum or the moisturizer, so she orders a few hundred boxes with a design she can afford to abandon. If the serum takes off, she reorders more of the same and quietly drops the rest. If she had been forced into a five-thousand-unit minimum, she would be stuck holding boxes for a product nobody wanted, or worse, shipping a subpar box to every customer for the next year because she could not justify buying more. The low minimum is not a discount. It is what lets the packaging stay honest to what the brand actually is at that moment.

## What makes an unboxing worth sharing

Not every custom box earns the moment. Plenty of brands print a logo on a mailer and call it done, and that lands about as flat as a plain brown box with extra steps. The ones that get filmed and shared tend to do a few specific things.

They control the reveal. A magnetic rigid box that opens slowly, or a drawer that slides out, builds a beat of anticipation. That pause is the difference between opening mail and opening a gift. The structure is doing the storytelling before a single word of copy appears.

They use restraint. The strongest unboxing experiences in 2026 are not the loudest. One well-placed insert, one deliberate detail, reads as intentional. A box stuffed with flyers and stickers reads as anxious. Innova Market Insights named "Sensory Unboxing" a top packaging trend for the year, and the point is not more stuff in the box. It is a tactile experience that feels considered.

They survive shipping. This is the unglamorous part that quietly separates brands. A beautiful box that arrives crushed is a worse outcome than a plain box that arrives intact. The structure has to protect the product through a journey the brand never sees. Design and durability are the same problem, not two.

They match the screen. When the box, the website, and the product photography all speak the same visual language, the brand feels cohesive. A premium-looking box that does not match a cheap-looking site reads as a costume. Consistency is what turns a nice box into a signal the customer can trust.

There is also a compounding effect worth naming. Unboxing videos with that word in the title have racked up more than 25 billion views on YouTube, and the majority of shoppers now say social media shapes their buying decisions. When a customer films your box and posts it, they are doing your most expensive marketing job for free. That only happens if the opening is worth filming, which is why the sensory details, the slow reveal, the one surprising insert, matter far out of proportion to what they cost.

The through-line in all of this is that the unboxing is not a marketing gimmick bolted on at the end. It is the physical half of a brand that lives mostly online, and it has to earn its keep like every other channel.
