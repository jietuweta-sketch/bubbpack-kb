# Low MOQ Custom Packaging: A Practical Guide for Small DTC Brands

A DTC founder I know sells candles. Sixty a month, maybe 120 in a good month. She wanted custom boxes with her logo, her colors, and a proper insert. The first supplier she called quoted a minimum order of 3,000 units. That was more boxes than she'd move in two years, so she shipped candles in plain mailers for another year while the brands next to her on the shelf unboxed like a film title sequence.

This is the wall most small brands hit. The custom packaging market reached $39.7 billion in 2025 and is projected to hit $59.6 billion by 2033, according to Grand View Research. But most of that spend belongs to established brands that can absorb a 3,000-unit run without flinching. The small players got locked out, not because they didn't care about packaging, but because the unit economics were built for someone else.

That's changing. Low-minimum-order printing has quietly opened custom packaging to brands that sell dozens, not thousands. This guide is for them.

## Why minimums exist, and why they punish small brands

Minimum order quantities aren't greed, exactly. Setting up a flexo press costs a factory the same whether it prints 500 boxes or 50,000. The fixed cost spreads thinner as volume climbs, so suppliers push larger orders to protect their margins. On paper that's reasonable.

The damage shows up in a small brand's cash flow and risk. A startup that commits to 3,000 boxes ties up a few thousand dollars in packaging before selling a single unit. If the design needs a tweak in three months, those 3,000 boxes are scrap. If the brand pivots, same result. High minimums leave small brands with three bad options: over-order and sit on inventory, settle for blank stock boxes, or skip branding entirely.

There's a better path now. Digital printing and short-run converting have driven setup costs down enough that suppliers can turn a profit on runs of a few hundred boxes, sometimes even fewer. The same forces that made print-on-demand books viable are reshaping packaging. For a brand moving 60 units a month, a 200-box order is an investment you can actually justify.

## Your box is the cheapest marketing channel you own

Here's the part that convinces me packaging is underpriced as a channel. The numbers around unboxing are hard to ignore. Roughly 40% of consumers have shared an unboxing on social media, and 67% of shoppers say premium packaging drove a repeat purchase, per industry tracking compiled by ZEE Custom Boxes. Modpac's research puts it more bluntly: 62% of people who watch unboxing videos are doing it to research a product before buying.

Think about what that means. Every box you ship is a tiny ad that lands in a customer's hands, in their home, in front of their phone camera. A boring mailer gets opened and tossed. A well-designed box with a considered insert gets photographed, filmed, and shown to people who will never visit your site. That's earned media you don't pay a platform for.

For a small brand, this matters more than for a big one. You don't have a retail endcap or a six-figure influencer budget. The box is the one physical touchpoint you control completely, from the first cut of the tape to the last layer of tissue. It's the cheapest way to look like you belong next to brands that outspend you a hundred to one.

There's a line to respect here, though. The same consumers who film their unboxing also notice when a tiny product arrives in a box stuffed with three layers of void fill and a marketing brochure the size of a magazine. The sustainability backlash is real, and small brands can't afford to look wasteful. The fix isn't austerity, it's intention. A tight-fitting box with a recyclable insert reads as care. A half-empty box packed with plastic reads as overhead. Aim for the former and your unboxing moment doubles as a values signal instead of a liability.

## What to actually look for in a small-batch supplier

Not every supplier that says "low MOQ" means it. When you're comparing options, focus on four things.

**The real minimum, not the advertised one.** Some suppliers list 500 as their floor, then bury a setup fee that only makes sense at 2,000. Ask for the all-in price at the quantity you'll actually order, including plates, die-cutting, and shipping. A low MOQ with a high setup charge is just a high MOQ wearing a disguise.

**Print quality on short runs.** Digital printing handles fine detail well, but colors can shift from what you approved on screen. Request a physical proof before a full run. It costs a little and saves you from 200 boxes in the wrong shade of green.

**Structure, not just graphics.** A box is a piece of engineering before it's a canvas. The flap geometry, the insert, the way the product sits when the lid opens, that's what makes an unboxing feel premium or flimsy. Look for a supplier who can advise on structure, not just print your logo on a stock template.

**Sustainable materials without the premium.** Recyclable paper and board are table stakes now, not a differentiator, and brands shouldn't pay luxury markups for them. Check that the supplier's default stock is FSC-certified or at least recyclable, so your unboxing doesn't come with a guilt tax.

## Start small, ship, and iterate

You don't need to nail the box on the first try. Order a short run, ship it to real customers, and watch what happens. Do people photograph it? Do repeat orders climb? Do fewer items come back because the insert protected the product properly? The data from 50 real shipments beats any amount of pre-launch agonizing over a render.
