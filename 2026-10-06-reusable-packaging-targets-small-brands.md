# Reusable Packaging Targets Are Coming: What Small Brands Should Do Before 2030

Copenhagen now charges a 5 DKK deposit on every takeaway cup and container. You tap your card at one of 54 reverse vending machines, drop the cup in, and the deposit lands back in your account through Mastercard Move. No coins, no cash, no staff. The city rolled this out in April 2026 with a Danish company called New Loop, and it is only the beginning.

There are roughly 55 billion takeaway packages used in Europe every year. Most of them get used once and thrown away. Regulators are done waiting for brands to fix this voluntarily.

## The target you can't ignore

The EU's Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR) puts a number on it. By 1 January 2030, 10% of alcoholic and non-alcoholic beverages have to be sold in reusable packaging that operates within a return system. By 2040 that number jumps to 40%.

Ten percent sounds small until you realize it is a legal floor, not a suggestion. Amcor, one of the biggest packaging suppliers on the planet, started warning beverage producers in July 2026 to rethink their closures and bottle choices now, because reuse changes what the packaging has to survive. A reusable bottle goes through washing chemicals, temperature swings, and repeated filling. The cap that works once on a single-use bottle may not hold up on cycle number twenty.

I keep coming back to that detail because it is the part nobody talks about. Reuse is not a material swap. It is a different product with different physics, and it has quietly arrived while most small brands were still arguing about paper versus plastic.

## Why Germany makes it work and most trials didn't

Germany is the poster child every regulator points to. Return rates there are astonishing: 95% of refillable glass bottles come back, 99% of refillable PET, and 98% of single-use drink containers. When the deposit is meaningful and the return point is around the corner, people actually return the bottle.

The inconvenient part is that most other reuse trials have failed. Reuters ran a piece in July 2026 bluntly titled about reuse getting "another shot," and the history it lays out is not flattering. Early schemes collapsed because consumers never bothered. France is throwing 50 million euros a year at reuse and refill infrastructure just to keep the idea alive. Copenhagen is betting that instant digital deposits will fix the takeup problem that sank the last round.

Here is what I actually think is going on. Reuse works when the infrastructure is invisible. Germany built deposit returns into daily life over decades. Copenhagen is trying to compress that into a tap-and-go experience. A small brand cannot build either of those things on its own, and it should not try.

## What this actually means for small brands

You are probably not Amcor's customer. You are running a skincare line or a coffee brand or a DTC food business with a few thousand units a month. The 2030 target applies to beverages first, but the direction of travel is obvious. Reuse and refill will creep into cosmetics, household goods, and food, and the pressure will come from two sides at once: regulation and retailers who want shelf space to look circular.

That puts small brands in an uncomfortable spot. The big players can pilot return systems with Le Fourgon or build stainless-steel deposit programs the way Nestlé is testing with KitKat. A small brand can't absorb the cost of washing, reverse logistics, and a consumer-facing return app. But small brands also can't sit this out and hope it stays in the beverage aisle.

The realistic move is not to build a return system. It is to design packaging that is ready for whichever system wins in your market. That means a few specific things:

1. Know whether your primary container is even reusable. A thin mailer box that tears when opened is not. A rigid box with a coated finish might be.
2. Make the outer packaging refill-friendly, not just recyclable. If a customer keeps a jar and only reorders a refill pouch, the pouch is now your packaging problem, and its recyclability suddenly matters more.
3. Keep your runs small enough to change fast. The brands that get hurt by the 2030 shift are the ones sitting on a warehouse full of packaging that no longer complies. Small batch production is how you stay nimble.

That last point is the one that actually matters for most readers of this. If your minimum order quantity forces you to buy a year's worth of boxes at once, you are locked in. When the reuse rules land in your category, you either eat that inventory or run it anyway and hope nobody notices. Neither is good.

## What to do now, while it is still early

Don't rebuild your packaging today around a rule that might change. Do start asking your supplier better questions. Can you order fewer units? Can the design move to a refill insert later without re-engineering the whole box? Is the material you picked actually accepted in the return streams being built in your biggest market?
