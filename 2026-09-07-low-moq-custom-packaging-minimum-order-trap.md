# Low MOQ Custom Packaging: The Minimum-Order Trap That Kills DTC Launches

There's a conversation that happens a few times a week in the DTC world, and it ends the same way every time. A founder with a finished product and a working Shopify store calls a packaging factory for a quote. They want maybe 300 boxes to test the market. The factory comes back with a 5,000-unit minimum. When the founder asks why, the answer is a shrug and a line about "setup costs." So they order 5,000 boxes of a product nobody has bought yet, and six months later they're paying storage fees on 3,800 of them.

This is the minimum-order trap, and it has killed more launches than bad branding ever did.

## Why the 5,000-unit wall exists

The first thing to understand is that the minimum isn't a scam. It's real economics, and it's worth knowing because it explains when the wall actually applies and when it's just inertia.

A custom folding carton carries a few fixed costs that don't change whether you run 100 units or 10,000. Someone has to cut the dieline, mix the ink, and prep the plates. Spread across 10,000 boxes, that setup is a rounding error. Spread across 300, it's a meaningful slice of every box. A traditional offset printer, built around long runs, wants that setup amortized over as many units as possible. So they set a floor, usually 5,000, sometimes 10,000, and they'd rather lose a small order than touch a short run.

None of that is greed. It's a factory optimized for volume, and volume is where their margin lives. The problem is what that floor does to the brand on the other end of the phone.

When a new brand commits to 5,000 units, they're making three bets at once. They're betting the product sells. They're betting the design doesn't need changes after real customers handle it. And they're betting the packaging won't need a legal or formula change before the boxes run out. Lose any one of those bets and you're left with a pallet of expensive recycling.

## Digital printing broke the floor for paper

Here's what changed over the last couple of years. The 5,000-unit wall was built on offset printing and machine finishing, where setup is the whole cost story. Digital printing doesn't work that way.

On a digital press, setup is measured in minutes, not plates. A folding carton can go from file to finished box in hours, and a 250-unit run stops being a favor and becomes a normal job. The same goes for short-run finishing: foil, die-cutting, and even spot UV now run on equipment that doesn't demand a 5,000-unit setup to make economic sense.

This is why you're seeing suppliers quote 250 to 500 units on folding cartons, 200 to 300 on rigid boxes, and a few hundred on printed mailers and tissue. It's the same box, same finish, same quality, produced in a quantity that matches where the brand actually is.

The unit price is higher, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something. A 250-unit box costs more per box than a 5,000-unit box. But that framing misses the point. The real question is how much cash you're locking into a pallet before you know the product sells. A 250-unit run might cost a few hundred dollars all in. A 5,000-unit run is a four-figure check, and the gap between those two numbers is your launch marketing budget.

## Small batches are now a compliance decision

There's a second reason the big-run habit is getting riskier, and it has nothing to do with cash flow.

Label regulations are moving. In cosmetics, the Modernization of Cosmetics Regulation Act (MoCRA) is forcing fragrance-allergen and INCI disclosures that have pushed many indie skincare brands onto 12-to-18-month label-revision cycles. The EU's Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR) is phasing in recyclability and recycled-content rules through 2030 and 2040. When a label revision or formula change lands, inventory printed with the old label becomes scrap. It can't be sold as-is.

This is where the small-run math flips in a way that surprises people. A 5,000-unit run is cheaper per box. But if a regulation forces a label change before you sell through, that cheap inventory turns into a liability. A 200 or 500 unit run is a cost you can walk away from. The five-thousand-unit pallet is a write-off you have to explain to someone.

Sustainability plays a role here too, and it's no longer a marketing line. A Shorr survey found 54% of US shoppers chose a product with sustainable packaging in the past six months, and 39% switched brands over it. Recyclable, mono-material packaging, no plastic windows, soy-based inks: these are the baseline now, not the premium tier. The catch is that sustainable materials often carry even higher minimums at a traditional factory, because converters are set up for volume. Small-batch digital runs are frequently the only realistic way to put a genuinely recyclable box on a product you're launching for the first time.

## What to actually do

The playbook that's emerged for 2026 is simple. Prototype fast, run a few hundred units, sell them, learn, then scale the winner.

That ordering matters because most founders do it backwards. They scale first and learn second, and they pay for the lesson in storage fees and closeout discounts.

A small first run is a paid experiment that produces a sellable product. You put real packaging in front of real customers, get real feedback, and find out whether the design and formula actually hold up before you commit to a run that could fill a garage. If something needs to change, you change it on the next run and it costs almost nothing. If you'd ordered 5,000, that change costs you a pallet.
